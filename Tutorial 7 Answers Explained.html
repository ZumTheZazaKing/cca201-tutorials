Q1 · Preventing deadlock, condition by condition

a) Mutual exclusion
Answer · line 1Bypass Mechanism: Implemented by using spooling for shared I/O devices (such as a printer), allowing jobs to write output to a high-speed disk buffer instead of waiting for direct exclusive hardware access.
In simple wordsMutual exclusion = only one job can use the printer at a time. Spooling avoids it: each job writes its pages into a waiting area on the disk (the spool) and continues. Nobody waits in front of the printer, so nobody holds it while waiting.
Answer · line 2Complication / Disadvantage: Some resources inherently require exclusive allocation and cannot be spooled or shared safely (and if the spooling disk area fills up completely before any job finishes spooling, a spooling deadlock can still occur).
In simple wordsTwo problems. 1) Not everything can be spooled: a scanner or a file being edited must really be used by one job at a time. 2) The spool area has a limited size: if it fills up while every job is only half written, the printer cannot start any job, and everyone is stuck again.
b) Resource holding (hold and wait)
Answer · line 3Bypass Mechanism: Jobs are required to request and be allocated every necessary resource at creation time before execution begins.
In simple wordsHold and wait = a job keeps what it has while it waits for more. The fix: a job must get everything it needs before it starts. If it cannot have everything, it waits holding nothing.
Answer · line 4Complication / Disadvantage: The degree of multiprogramming is significantly decreased, and peripheral devices remain idle for long periods while waiting for a job to finish using other resources.
In simple wordsBecause each job grabs all its devices at the start, fewer jobs can run at the same time (less multiprogramming). And a device can sit unused: a job that prints only at the very end still keeps the printer from the beginning.
c) No preemption
Answer · line 5Bypass Mechanism: The operating system is permitted to forcibly deallocate (preempt) resources from executing jobs when needed.
In simple wordsNo preemption = nobody can take a resource away from a job. The fix: allow the OS to take it back by force and give it to another job.
Answer · line 6Complication / Disadvantage: While acceptable when a job's state can be easily saved and restored (like CPU registers), preempting dedicated I/O devices or files mid-modification results in extremely unpleasant and complex recovery tasks.
In simple wordsFor the CPU it is easy: the OS saves the registers and restores them later (this happens all the time). For a printer halfway through a document, or a file halfway through an update, taking it away leaves a mess: half-printed pages, broken files, work to redo.
d) Circular wait
Answer · line 7Bypass Mechanism: The operating system enforces a hierarchical ordering scheme on all resource types, preventing circular chains from forming.
In simple wordsCircular wait = A waits for B, B waits for C, C waits for A. The fix: give every resource type a number (printer = 1, disk = 2, …) and force every job to ask in increasing order. Everyone climbs the numbers in the same direction, so a circle can never close.
Answer · line 8Complication / Disadvantage: It requires jobs to anticipate their exact resource request order in advance, making it difficult to satisfy the dynamic needs of all users.
In simple wordsA job must know in advance in which order it will need things. Real programs often decide during execution, so the fixed order is not always practical, and a job may have to book a resource early, before it really needs it.
✍ Exam tip. Write the answer as 4 blocks with the same 2 lines each: Bypass (how the condition becomes false) and Complication (what it costs). A table with 4 rows and 2 columns is perfect.

Q2 · The Banker's algorithm

1) Needs and available
Answer · line 1Total Allocated = 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 9 units
In simple wordsAdd up what every job holds right now.
Answer · line 2Available Units = 12 − 9 = 3 units
In simple wordsWhat is left in the pool = total − allocated. Only these 3 units can be lent now.
Answer · line 3Remaining Needs (Maximum Required − Allocated): J1: 6 − 2 = 4 · J2: 5 − 3 = 2 · J3: 9 − 2 = 7 · J4: 4 − 2 = 2
In simple wordsRemaining need = how many more units the job may still ask for = maximum − what it already has. Always present it as a table: Job | Allocated | Maximum | Need.
2) Is it safe?
Answer · line 4Yes, the system is in a safe state.
In simple wordsSafe = there is at least one order in which every job can get its maximum, finish, and give everything back.
Answer · line 5J2 (or J4) requires 2 units (≤ 3 available). J2 runs to completion and releases its 3 allocated units ⟹ Available = 3 + 3 = 6.
In simple wordsLook for a job whose need fits in the available units: J2 needs 2 and 3 are available. Imagine J2 gets them, finishes, and returns everything it holds (its 3 units; the 2 lent units come back too, so we just add its allocation): 3 + 3 = 6.
Answer · line 6J4 requires 2 units (≤ 6 available). J4 runs to completion and releases its 2 allocated units ⟹ Available = 6 + 2 = 8.
In simple wordsSame move: J4 needs 2 ≤ 6, it finishes and gives back its 2: 6 + 2 = 8.
Answer · line 7J1 requires 4 units (≤ 8 available). J1 runs to completion and releases its 2 allocated units ⟹ Available = 8 + 2 = 10.
In simple wordsJ1 needs 4 ≤ 8, finishes, gives back 2: 10.
Answer · line 8J3 requires 7 units (≤ 10 available). J3 runs to completion and releases its 2 allocated units ⟹ Available = 10 + 2 = 12.
In simple wordsThe biggest need comes last, when the pool is large enough: 7 ≤ 10. At the end we are back to 12: everything returned.
Answer · line 9Safe Sequence: ⟨J2, J4, J1, J3⟩ (or ⟨J4, J2, J1, J3⟩).
In simple wordsWrite the order in angle brackets. Other orders also work, as long as each step respects “need ≤ available” (for example ⟨J2, J1, J3, J4⟩). The first job must be J2 or J4: they are the only ones whose need fits in 3.
3) J3 asks for 2 more units
Answer · line 10Decision: The operating system must block (deny) the request.
In simple wordsThe answer first, then the proof.
Answer · line 11J3 requesting 2 units, the OS blocks it because only 1 unit remains available while every job needs ≥ 2;
In simple wordsThe method: (1) 2 ≤ J3's need (7)? yes. (2) 2 ≤ available (3)? yes. (3) Pretend to give the 2 units and check if the new state is still safe.
Answer · line 12Total allocated becomes 11, leaving 1 available unit. Since every job requires at least 2 remaining units (J1 = 4, J2 = 2, J3 = 5, J4 = 2), no job can complete. The system would enter an unsafe state, so the OS blocks the request.
In simple wordsAfter pretending: allocated 9 + 2 = 11, available 12 − 11 = 1. J3's need drops from 7 to 5. Now the smallest need is 2 but only 1 unit is free: no job can finish, so the state is unsafe. The OS refuses, J3 waits, and the system stays in the safe state.
4) A practical limitation
Answer · line 13Because the algorithm assumes the worst-case scenario (that all jobs will simultaneously demand their maximum declared resources), resources are kept reserved and underutilized.
In simple wordsThe banker always plans for the worst: “what if everybody asks for their maximum at the same time?”. Usually that never happens, so units stay unused just in case.
Answer · line 14Consequently, jobs are kept waiting unnecessarily even when physical units are idle, causing overall system scheduling and throughput to suffer.
In simple wordsExample from part 3: J3 had to wait although 3 units were physically free. Fewer jobs finish per hour (lower throughput).
Answer · line 15(Additionally, it requires a fixed number of jobs and a constant total number of resources, which is unrealistic in dynamic server environments.)
In simple wordsIt also needs every job to declare its maximum in advance, and the number of jobs and units must not change. On a real server, jobs arrive and leave and hardware can fail.
✍ Exam tip. Always show the table and one line per step: “J… needs … ≤ …, finishes, available = … + … = …”. Marks are given for the trace, not only for the final word “safe”.

Q3 · Deadlock detection by graph reduction

1) Reduction
Answer · line 1Step 1: P4 holds R4 and is not waiting for any resource. Remove P4 and release its instance of R4 back to the available pool.
In simple wordsStart with a process that waits for nothing: it can finish. Remove it from the graph and free what it held.
Answer · line 2Step 2: P2 is waiting for R4. Since R4 is now available, P2 acquires R4, completes execution, and releases R2 and R4.
In simple wordsFreeing R4 unblocks P2. P2 finishes and frees R2 (and R4).
Answer · line 3Step 3: P1 acquires the newly freed R2, finishes execution, and releases R1.
In simple wordsChain reaction: P1 was waiting for R2.
Answer · line 4Step 4: P3 acquires R1, finishes execution, and releases R3.
In simple wordsAnd P3 was waiting for R1.
Answer · line 5Conclusion: All connecting lines can be removed; the system is not deadlocked.
In simple wordsIf every process can be removed, there is no deadlock. Order of removal: P4, P2, P1, P3.
2) P4 requests R2
Answer · line 6Reduction Attempt: All four processes are waiting for resources held by other processes: P1 waits for R2, held by P2. P2 waits for R4, held by P4. P3 waits for R1, held by P1. P4 waits for R2, held by P2.
In simple wordsMake a list “who waits for what, held by whom”. Now nobody waits for nothing: there is no starting point for the reduction.
Answer · line 7No process can complete and release its allocated resource. Therefore, no process can be removed from the graph.
In simple wordsThe reduction stops at step 0: every process is blocked.
Answer · line 8Conclusion: The system is deadlocked. P2 and P4 form a circular wait (P2 → R4 → P4 → R2 → P2).
In simple wordsFind the circle: P2 waits for P4's resource and P4 waits for P2's resource. That small circle is the deadlock.
Answer · line 9P1 and P3 are also blocked because they depend on resources held by deadlocked processes. Thus, all four processes are unable to proceed.
In simple wordsP1 and P3 are not in the circle, but they wait (directly or through each other) for resources held by P2. They are stuck because of the circle. Name all four in your answer, and say which two form the circle.
✍ Exam tip. Draw the graph: arrow resource → holder, dashed arrow process → resource it waits for. Then write one line per removal. In part 2, separate “in the circle” from “blocked by the circle”.

Q4 · Choosing a victim

1) The victim
Answer · line 1Selected Victim: P2.
In simple wordsGive the answer first.
Answer · line 2Job Priority: High-priority jobs are usually left untouched. P1 holds a higher priority (2) than P2 and P3 (3), making P1 less favorable for termination.
In simple wordsCareful: 1 = highest, so 2 is more important than 3. P1 is the most important of the three: keep it.
Answer · line 3CPU Time Used: Jobs close to completion are usually left alone. P1 has already completed 18 ms of its 20 ms execution (90% finished), whereas P2 has only used 3 ms out of 15 ms (20% finished), minimizing wasted CPU computation if P2 is restarted.
In simple wordsKilling a job throws away the work already done. P1 is 90% done: killing it wastes 18 ms. P2 is 20% done: only 3 ms lost.
Answer · line 4Jobs Modifying Data: Jobs actively modifying database records are avoided because terminating them causes data corruption and complex database rollback issues. P3 is updating a database, eliminating it as a safe candidate.
In simple wordsStopping a database update in the middle can leave half-written records. P3 is excluded for this reason.
Answer · line 5Number of Other Jobs Affected: Terminating P3 would disrupt 2 dependent downstream jobs, whereas terminating P2 affects 0 other jobs while still breaking the circular wait.
In simple wordsKilling P3 would also hurt 2 other jobs. Killing P2 hurts nobody else, and it still breaks the circle.
2) Recovery without a victim
Answer · line 6The operating system can stop new jobs from entering the system, temporarily interrupt/suspend deadlocked jobs with a saved snapshot of their progress (or preempt resources from a non-deadlocked job whose state can be saved and restored), and allow non-deadlocked jobs to finish and release their resources so the deadlocked processes can complete without any victim being terminated.
In simple wordsFour steps: (1) stop new jobs from coming in; (2) pause a deadlocked job and save a snapshot (checkpoint) of its progress, or take resources from a job whose state can be saved; (3) let the other jobs finish and give back their resources; (4) the stuck jobs continue, the paused one resumes from its snapshot. Nobody is killed.
✍ Exam tip. Use the 4 factors as 4 short paragraphs: priority, CPU time used, modifying data, other jobs affected. For each one, say which process it protects. The victim is the one no factor protects.

Q5 · Starvation in the ring of threads

1) The hazard
Answer · line 1Hazard: Starvation (indefinite postponement).
In simple wordsThe name to write: starvation, also called indefinite postponement.
Answer · line 2Root Cause vs. Deadlock: Starvation results from conservative or unfair resource allocation, where a job's execution is prevented because it waits for resources that never become simultaneously available while other jobs continuously make progress.
In simple wordsT2 needs both of its locks at once. T1 holds one of them, then T3 holds the other, then T1 again… so the two locks are never free together. The policy “give both or nothing” is safe, but unfair to T2. Meanwhile T1 and T3 keep working.
Answer · line 3In contrast, a deadlock occurs when processes are locked in a mutual circular wait where none of the involved processes can proceed.
In simple wordsThe key difference: in a deadlock nobody in the circle progresses. In starvation the system progresses; only one thread is left behind.
2) Detection and resolution
Answer · line 4Detection (Aging): The operating system implements an aging algorithm that tracks the exact duration each job has spent waiting for its required resources.
In simple wordsTo detect, the OS must measure: it records how long each thread has been waiting.
Answer · line 5Resolution: Once T2's waiting time crosses a predefined threshold and starvation is detected, the operating system temporarily blocks new incoming jobs (or new lock requests from competing threads) from acquiring resources until the starving thread (T2) has had its resource request satisfied and completes its execution.
In simple wordsWhen the wait is longer than a limit (the threshold), the OS stops giving T2's locks to T1 and T3. They finish their current work and release the locks; now both are free together, T2 takes them and runs. Then normal allocation resumes.
✍ Exam tip. Two parts in your answer: (1) name + root cause + one sentence comparing with deadlock; (2) detection (measure waiting time, threshold) and resolution (block the others until the starving thread is served).
